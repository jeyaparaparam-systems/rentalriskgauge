How to read this

Each score runs from 0 to 100. Lower is better. A low score means calm conditions for rental owners. A high score means several things are going wrong at once.

Each score has six parts. The two worst parts count extra, so one serious problem can't hide behind calm readings elsewhere.

0–35Low

Calm. Loans are affordable, people have jobs and rentals are in demand.

35–45Moderate

Some pressure. One or two things are working against you, but nothing is breaking.

45–65Elevated

Caution. Several warning signs at once. In the past this came before slower rent growth or tighter lending.

65–100High

Stress. Most things point the wrong way. This happened in 2008–09, briefly in early 2002 and, for apartment buildings (5+ units), in 2003–06.

  • Watch the trendA rise of 10 points over a few weeks matters more than any single day.
  • High doesn't mean "sell"In past cycles, prices were often lowest when the score was high.
  • Look at the parts"What's driving the score" on the dashboard shows which numbers push it up.

How the scores work

The scores show whether rental owners in the San Francisco Bay Area face normal conditions or extra pressure.

Data. We use five licensed sources where they are more detailed or more local than free data. Four have a free public backup, used whenever the licensed number is missing:

  • Case-Shiller home prices → FHFA home prices
  • Moody's Baa credit spread → U.S. Treasury corporate bond spread
  • CoStar apartment data → Census rental vacancy, the rent price index (rent paid) and price-to-rent
  • MLS months of supply → Realtor.com listings per home under contract
  • Nasdaq-100 → no backup. Without it, the other measures in its part count more.

Licensed data is shown for non-commercial use while we ask the providers for permission. To see the free-data version, add ?data=public to the end of the web address.

What this is, and what it isn't

An independent indicator. Not connected to any lender, broker, government agency or data provider.

It is

  • A snapshot of the pressure on the rental market today.
  • Built from published data with fixed rules, all listed on this page.
  • A way to compare today with the past on the same scale.

It isn't

  • A prediction of prices, rents or rates.
  • An appraisal of any property.
  • A lending standard.
  • Advice to buy, sell, borrow or refinance.

A high score doesn't mean prices will fall, and a low score doesn't mean they will rise. It counts how many of the conditions that hurt owners in the past are present today.

Our rules

  • Every measure, level and weight is published below.
  • Every number has a listed source on the Sources page.
  • The rules are the same every day. If we change them, we update this page and recalculate the history.
  • The rules reflect our judgment. We test them against history below, but no one has audited them.

From numbers to a score

1. Score each measure from 0 to 100. Each one has a calm level (0) and a stress level (100). A reading halfway between scores 50.

Example: calm is 3%, stress is 7%. A reading of 5% scores 50.

2. Group measures into parts. For example, "Mortgage financing" combines today's rate (70%) and its change from a year ago (30%).

3. Combine the parts. 75% comes from the weighted average of all six parts. 25% comes from the average of the two worst parts, so one serious problem can't hide behind calm readings. In 2008 a plain average reached only about 57 while jobs, prices and credit were all collapsing.

Example: weighted average 49, two worst parts 76 and 69: 0.75 × 49 + 0.25 × 72.5 = 54.9.

If a number is missing, it is left out and the rest of its part counts more. A missing number never counts as calm or stressed.

Single-family homes & condos

The single-family score has six parts, built from up to 13 measures. Each measure is scored between its low-risk and high-risk level. The table shows the measures in use today.

Why these parts

  • Mortgage financing looks at today's rate (70%) and how much it has changed over the past year (30%), because a fast jump hurts more than a slow one.
  • Market & credit stress combines the VIX, averaged over the past 20 market days so a one-day scare does not swing the score (50%), the Moody's Baa corporate bond spread (25%; the extra interest riskier companies pay to borrow compared with the 10-year Treasury) and how far the Nasdaq-100 is below its high of the past year (25%). Many San Francisco Bay Area workers are paid partly in tech stock, so a deep tech sell-off affects what they can spend on housing. Free fallback: the U.S. Treasury high-grade corporate bond spread, with the VIX and spread re-weighted.
  • Jobs combines the unemployment rate (50%; the San Francisco and San Jose metro rates, seasonally adjusted and weighted by the size of each labor force), tech jobs compared with a year ago (30%) and all jobs compared with a year ago (20%). Unemployment tends to rise only after layoffs have happened; job counts usually turn down earlier. Tech jobs alone can miss broad downturns (they kept growing in 2008–09 and in 2020), so we include total jobs too.
  • Homes for sale combines months of supply for single-family homes in Santa Clara County from MLS data (50%), listings compared with a year ago (25%) and the share of listings with a price cut (25%), the last two from Realtor.com. Free fallback for months of supply: active listings per pending sale (Realtor.com).
  • Home prices is the change over the past year, half from the Case-Shiller San Francisco index and half from the FHFA index for San Jose–Sunnyvale–Santa Clara. Case-Shiller's "San Francisco" area doesn't include Santa Clara County, so the San Jose half keeps the South Bay in the score. Free backup for the San Francisco half: the FHFA indexes for San Francisco–San Mateo, Oakland–Berkeley and San Jose.
  • Valuation (price-to-rent) compares home prices with rents paid in the San Francisco–Oakland area, as a share of the 2005–2025 average (100 = average). Half uses Case-Shiller San Francisco prices, half FHFA San Jose prices. It shows whether prices have run ahead of rents. It was highest in 2005–06, before the last major downturn.

What would push the single-family score higher?

  • Mortgage rates above 7.75%, especially if still a full point higher than a year ago.
  • San Francisco Bay Area unemployment above 5.5%, or tech jobs down more than 3% from a year earlier.
  • The Baa corporate bond spread above 2.5 points, the VIX staying above 30, or tech stocks more than 20% below their high. All have happened in past downturns.
  • More than 3 months of supply in Santa Clara County, or more than 40% of listings with a price cut.
  • Home prices falling compared with a year ago for two quarters or more.

How often does this data change?

The VIX, the Baa spread and the Nasdaq-100 change every market day, and mortgage rates are published weekly. Jobs, homes for sale and Case-Shiller prices are monthly (Case-Shiller about two months behind). On most days, only the market-stress and financing parts move.

Apartment buildings (5+ units)

The apartment score also has six parts, built from up to 11 measures. It leans more on loan costs, vacancy and rents than the single-family score does.

Why these parts

  • Apartment financing combines the 10-year Treasury yield (60%), the share of large banks tightening their standards for apartment loans in the Federal Reserve's loan officer survey (20%), and the Moody's Baa corporate bond spread (20%; free fallback: the U.S. Treasury high-grade spread). Rates tell you what a loan costs; the survey and the spread tell you how willing lenders are to make one.
  • Value cushion is the average San Francisco Bay Area apartment cap rate from CoStar (a building's yearly income divided by its price) minus the 10-year Treasury yield. It scores 0 at a gap of 3 points or more and 100 at 0.5 points or less. Apartment loans are usually priced about 1.5 to 2 points above the Treasury yield, so once the gap falls below about 1.5 points, borrowing costs more than a building earns. Free fallback: price-to-rent.
  • Jobs is the same measure used in the single-family score.
  • Rental vacancy uses CoStar's San Francisco Bay Area apartment vacancy rate (70%) and its change from a year earlier (30%), published quarterly. Free fallback: the U.S. Census Bureau's yearly California rental vacancy rate, compared with its own 2005–2025 average.
  • Rent growth blends the change over the past year in average asking apartment rents across the San Francisco Bay Area (CoStar, 50%) with rent paid by all tenants in the San Francisco–Oakland area (consumer price index, 50%). Asking rents show the market for new leases; rent paid shows what owners actually collect. Without CoStar data, rent paid is used on its own. Not every owner can raise rents at market pace: California's AB 1482 and local rules such as San Jose's Apartment Rent Ordinance can limit increases.
  • New construction compares homes permitted over the past 24 months in the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas with the average 24-month pace since 2005 (100 = average). Permits come before construction, so this shows supply on the way. Permits cover all private housing, not only apartments.

What would push the apartment score higher?

  • The 10-year Treasury yield above 5.25%, or banks broadly tightening standards for apartment loans.
  • Rent growth slowing below 1% or turning negative.
  • San Francisco Bay Area apartment vacancy rising above 5.5%, or cap rates falling to within 0.5 points of the 10-year Treasury yield.
  • Building permits above 130 (30% faster than the usual pace).
  • San Francisco Bay Area unemployment above 5.5%, or tech jobs down more than 3%.

How often does this data change?

The 10-year Treasury yield and the Baa spread change every market day. Jobs and permits are monthly; CoStar cap rates, vacancy and asking rents and the bank survey are quarterly. On most days only the financing and value-cushion parts move.

Shown, but not scored

  • Layoff alert. Workers in California WARN layoff notices filed for San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Alameda and Contra Costa counties over the last 30 days. Large employers must give 60 days' notice before mass layoffs, so this is an early warning. It is not scored because notices vary widely in size and timing.
  • Insurance costs. The national price index for home and renters insurance compared with a year ago, shown in the indicator strip. It affects what it costs to run a rental, but it varies too much from one property to another to score fairly.
  • Today in context. Today's scores compared with every month since 2000 (the share of months that scored lower, the last time a score was this high, and the highest and lowest readings), plus how many points each score is from the next risk level. This uses the same history shown in the chart.
  • Rent-to-income. We looked for a monthly, region-wide measure of rents compared with renters' incomes and did not find a reliable public one. The price-to-rent part covers the related question of whether prices have run ahead of rents.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Some measures are updated by hand. CoStar cap rates, vacancy and asking rents and MLS months of supply come from quarterly and monthly market reports, so they change less often than the daily market measures. Each indicator shows the date of its latest reading.
  • Daily measures can jump on one day's news. The VIX is half of the market-stress part, which is 15% of the single-family score, so a one-day spike moves the score by only a few points.
  • Job numbers get revised. Unemployment is seasonally adjusted, but the source revises past months, often by a tenth of a point or two.
  • The price-cut share is seasonal. It usually rises in late summer and fall.
  • This is our own model. The levels and weights are our judgment, not an industry standard.

Looking back to 2000

We rebuilt both scores for every month since January 2000 using today's rules. Case-Shiller and Moody's data go back that far. CoStar, MLS and Nasdaq data don't, so older months use the free backups instead. Measures that didn't exist yet are left out, and the rest of their part counts more: listings start in 2017, the bank loan survey in 2013 and the high-grade bond spread in 2004.

Single-family homes & condos

  • Highest score: 67.5 (High), in April 2009. The score was High from October 2008 to May 2009, when jobs, prices and credit all turned against owners at once.
  • 2001–2003: about 45–66 (Elevated, briefly High). The dot-com bust. Tech jobs fell nearly 18% in a year and unemployment more than doubled. The score went from about 27 in January 2001 to 66 in February 2002. Mortgage rates then fell to record lows, and prices kept rising.
  • 2006–2007: about 44–59 (mostly Elevated). Jobs were strong, but prices were far above rents and starting to fall, and mortgage rates had risen.
  • 2013–2019: mostly 10–28 (Low). Calm markets, low mortgage rates, strong job growth and rising prices. The lowest reading was about 10 in January 2018.
  • April 2020: about 50 (Elevated). Unemployment jumped above 13% during the COVID shutdown, while very low mortgage rates and rising prices offset part of the shock.
  • 2023 to now: mostly 38–56 (Moderate to Elevated). Mortgage rates more than doubled while prices fell, with a peak of about 56 in late 2023.

Apartment buildings (5+ units)

  • 2001–2002: rose from about 41 to 64 (Elevated) as tech jobs disappeared and rent growth stopped.
  • 2003–2006: about 60–68 (often High). San Francisco Bay Area rents fell for about two years, vacancy rose above normal and prices ran far ahead of rents.
  • 2009–2010: about 45–60 (Elevated), as job losses hit and rents fell.
  • 2012–2019: mostly 23–37 (Low). Rents rose quickly and vacancy fell. The lowest reading was about 23 in early 2015.
  • 2024: about 49–63 (Elevated), with high Treasury yields, slow growth in rent paid and stretched valuations.

The October 2026 monthly value is about 50, while today's daily score is about 44. Most of the gap comes from the data source: today's score uses CoStar's apartment vacancy (3.8%) and asking rents (+7.7%), which show a tighter market than the statewide vacancy and rent-paid stand-ins used in the history. The 10-year Treasury yield was also higher in October (about 5.1% on average).

What this tells us

High mortgage rates alone don't mean serious trouble. Jobs, credit and supply matter too. The 2008 crisis was the one period in the last two decades when almost every part turned against owners at the same time. The dot-com bust was mainly a jobs shock; falling mortgage rates kept home prices up, but apartment owners felt it for years.

How the risk levels were set

Both scores use the same four levels: Low under 35, Moderate 35–45, Elevated 45–65 and High 65 and up. We drew the lines where the past shows a real difference. Months under 35 were usually followed by rising prices. Months above 35 were followed by a price drop about a third of the time.

Since 2000, single-family homes & condos were Low in 112 months, Moderate in 79, Elevated in 124 and High in 7 (February 2002 and 2008–09). Apartment buildings (5+ units) were Low in 104 months, Moderate in 36, Elevated in 155 and High in 27 (2003–06, after rents fell in the dot-com bust). Elevated is common because the San Francisco Bay Area has had long stretches of real pressure: 2001–2004, 2006–2011 and again since 2022.

The "Compared with the past" card on the dashboard shows where today ranks against every month since 2000.

Does the score work?

We rebuilt the score for every month since 2000. Then we looked at what actually happened in the 12 months after each reading. This tells you how the score has behaved in the past. It is not a promise about the future.

  • San Jose too: we ran the same check against San Jose-area prices (FHFA). The match is a bit stronger there, and adding San Jose prices to the score improved it.
  • What it gets wrong: in 2002–03 the score was Elevated because of job losses, but mortgage rates fell to record lows and prices kept rising. It does not tell you when the bottom or top is.

Apartment buildings (5+ units): what rents did next

  • When the apartment score was Low, rent paid rose about 4.8% over the next year. When it was Elevated, rents rose only about 2.5%, and fell about 1 time in 7.
  • In High months (2003–06), rents rose only about 1.6% a year on average.

Does it depend on our weights?

We used AI automation to rerun today's scores 5,000 times, each time changing every weight up or down by as much as half. The single-family score stayed Elevated every time. The apartment score stayed Moderate about 4 times out of 5. It sits just below the Elevated line, so think of it as "Moderate, close to Elevated."

Keep in mind

  • We know how the story ended. We built the rules after seeing past downturns, and we used today's corrected data, not the numbers people had at the time. Real-time results would likely be a bit worse.
  • Part of the match is built in. Recent price and rent changes are part of the score, and those trends tend to keep going for a while.
  • Few big events to learn from. Since 2000 there have been two big downturns (the dot-com bust and 2007–2011), one sudden shock (2020) and one rate-driven slowdown (2022–23).
  • Areas don't match exactly. Prices cover San Francisco and San Jose, listings cover Santa Clara County, and jobs cover the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas.

Bottom line: the score is good at describing how much pressure the market is under right now. It can't tell you when prices will turn, so it isn't a forecast.
