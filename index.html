Understanding the Rental Risk Score

The Rental Risk Gauge uses a scale from 0 to 100.

A lower score means calmer market conditions. A higher score means greater market pressure.

Score calculation

75% of the weighted average across all six areas + 25% of the average of the two highest-risk areas.

This combines overall market conditions with extra emphasis on the areas under the most stress. The two highest-risk areas are already part of the weighted average, so this step deliberately gives them extra weight.

0 to under 35Low

Conditions are generally favorable. Financing is more manageable, employment is relatively stable and rental demand is holding up.

35 to under 45Moderate

Some warning signs are appearing. Certain costs or market conditions may be creating pressure, but the overall picture remains relatively manageable.

45 to under 65Elevated

Several areas are showing signs of stress. Owners may face higher financing costs, slower rent growth, weaker demand or other challenges.

65 to 100High

Market conditions are under significant pressure. Several important factors are working against property owners at the same time.

What Should You Watch?

  • Watch the direction, not just today's number.A score that rises steadily over several weeks may be more concerning than a single-day increase.
  • A high score does not automatically mean you should sell.It means more of the conditions that have created problems for property owners in the past are present.
  • Look at what is driving the score.Two markets can have the same overall score for very different reasons. Understanding the individual indicators gives you a clearer picture.

How We Calculate the Rental Risk Score

The score combines several market indicators into one number.

We look at six areas of the market, including financing, employment, property values, rents and vacancy.

Each indicator is converted to a score from 0 to 100 based on its normal and high-stress levels.

How the Final Score Is Calculated

The final score combines two things:

75% – Weighted average

The combined score across all six areas, giving more importance to the factors with higher assigned weights.

25% – Highest-risk areas

The average of the two areas showing the greatest stress.

This approach considers the overall market while giving extra attention to serious warning signs that might otherwise be hidden by healthier conditions elsewhere.

If data for an indicator is unavailable, the remaining available indicators are used. Missing information is not automatically treated as good or bad.

Show a worked example

1. Indicator score. Each indicator has a calm level (0) and a stress level (100). Example: calm is 3%, stress is 7%. A reading of 5% scores 50.

2. Area score. Related indicators are averaged by weight. Example: Mortgage financing = 70% today's rate + 30% its change from a year ago.

3. Final score.

Example: weighted average 49; two highest-risk areas 76 and 69 (average 72.5). Score = 0.75 × 49 + 0.25 × 72.5 = 54.9.

Why the extra weight? In 2008, a simple average reached only about 57 while jobs, prices and credit were all collapsing. Giving the two highest-risk areas extra weight keeps one severe problem from being hidden by calm readings elsewhere.

Figures shown on the dashboard are rounded. Scores are calculated from the unrounded values.

About the data and this model

Data. Most indicators come from free public data that is downloaded automatically. Apartment vacancy and rent on new leases come from Apartment List, and months of supply from Redfin, both free and updated monthly. We use four licensed sources where they are more detailed or more local than free data. Three have a free public backup, used whenever the licensed number is missing:

  • Case-Shiller home prices → FHFA home prices
  • Moody's Baa credit spread → U.S. Treasury corporate bond spread
  • CoStar apartment cap rate (entered by hand each quarter) → price-to-rent. Price-to-rent stands in for the cap-rate cushion but measures something different (see the apartment section).
  • Nasdaq-100 → no backup. Without it, the other indicators in its area carry more weight.

Licensed data is shown for non-commercial use while we ask the providers for permission. To see the free-data version, add ?data=public to the end of the web address.

What this is, and what it isn't

An independent indicator. Not connected to any lender, broker, government agency or data provider.

It is

  • A snapshot of the pressure on the rental market today.
  • Built from published data with fixed rules, all listed on this page.
  • A way to compare today with the past on the same scale.

It isn't

  • A prediction of prices, rents or rates.
  • An appraisal of any property.
  • A lending standard.
  • Advice to buy, sell, borrow or refinance.

A high score doesn't mean prices will fall, and a low score doesn't mean they will rise. It counts how many of the conditions that hurt owners in the past are present today.

Our rules

  • Every indicator, level and weight is published below.
  • Every number has a listed source on the Sources page.
  • The rules are the same every day. If we change them, we update this page and recalculate the history.
  • The rules reflect our judgment. We test them against history below, but no one has audited them.

What Drives the Single-Family Housing Score?

The single-family score looks at six areas that influence home values and housing market conditions.

These include mortgage rates, financial market conditions, employment, homes for sale, home prices and the relationship between home prices and rents.

Together, they help show whether conditions are becoming more favorable or more difficult for homeowners and real estate investors.

What Could Increase the Risk?

  • Mortgage rates rise significantly.
  • San Francisco Bay Area unemployment increases or technology jobs decline.
  • Credit becomes harder to obtain.
  • More homes sit on the market or sellers reduce asking prices.
  • Home prices begin falling.

How Often Does the Data Change?

Some indicators change daily or weekly, while employment, housing inventory and home price data generally update monthly.

The score changes as new information becomes available.

Show indicators, weights and levels

How each area is measured

  • Mortgage financing looks at today's rate (70%) and how much it has changed over the past year (30%), because a fast jump hurts more than a slow one.
  • Market & credit stress combines the VIX, averaged over the past 20 market days so a one-day scare does not swing the score (50%), the Moody's Baa corporate bond spread (25%; the extra interest riskier companies pay to borrow compared with the 10-year Treasury) and how far the Nasdaq-100 is below its high of the past year (25%). Many San Francisco Bay Area workers are paid partly in tech stock, so a deep tech sell-off affects what they can spend on housing. Free fallback: the U.S. Treasury high-grade corporate bond spread, with the VIX and spread re-weighted.
  • Jobs combines the unemployment rate (50%; the San Francisco and San Jose metro rates, seasonally adjusted and weighted by the size of each labor force), tech jobs compared with a year ago (30%) and all jobs compared with a year ago (20%). Unemployment tends to rise only after layoffs have happened; job counts usually turn down earlier. Tech jobs alone can miss broad downturns (they kept growing in 2008–09 and in 2020), so we include total jobs too.
  • Homes for sale combines months of supply for single-family homes in Santa Clara County from Redfin (50%), listings compared with a year ago (25%) and the share of listings with a price cut (25%), the last two from Realtor.com. Free fallback for months of supply: active listings per pending sale (Realtor.com).
  • Home prices is the change over the past year, half from the Case-Shiller San Francisco index and half from the FHFA index for San Jose–Sunnyvale–Santa Clara. Case-Shiller's "San Francisco" area doesn't include Santa Clara County, so the San Jose half keeps the South Bay in the score. Free backup for the San Francisco half: the FHFA indexes for San Francisco–San Mateo, Oakland–Berkeley and San Jose.
  • Valuation (price-to-rent) compares home prices with rents paid in the San Francisco–Oakland area, as a share of the 2005–2025 average (100 = average). Half uses Case-Shiller San Francisco prices, half FHFA San Jose prices. It shows whether prices have run ahead of rents. It was highest in 2005–06, before the last major downturn.

Levels that would push the score higher

  • Mortgage rates above 7.75%, especially if still a full point higher than a year ago.
  • San Francisco Bay Area unemployment above 5.5%, or tech jobs down more than 3% from a year earlier.
  • The Baa corporate bond spread above 2.5 points, the VIX staying above 30, or tech stocks more than 20% below their high. All have happened in past downturns.
  • More than 3 months of supply in Santa Clara County, or more than 40% of listings with a price cut.
  • Home prices falling compared with a year ago for two quarters or more.

What Drives the Apartment Building Score?

Apartment buildings face different financial pressures from single-family homes.

The apartment score focuses on six areas that directly affect apartment ownership: financing costs, property values, employment, vacancy, rent growth and new construction.

Together, these indicators help show whether apartment owners are facing increasing financial pressure.

What Could Increase the Risk?

  • Interest rates rise or banks tighten apartment lending.
  • Rent growth slows or turns negative.
  • Apartment vacancies increase.
  • Building values become less attractive compared with borrowing costs.
  • New housing construction increases competition for tenants.
  • San Francisco Bay Area employment weakens.

How Often Does the Data Change?

Interest rates and some financial indicators change daily. Employment, construction, apartment vacancy and rent data generally update monthly. Apartment cap rates update quarterly.

Show indicators, weights and levels

How each area is measured

  • Apartment financing combines the 10-year Treasury yield (60%), the share of large banks tightening their standards for apartment loans in the Federal Reserve's loan officer survey (20%), and the Moody's Baa corporate bond spread (20%; free fallback: the U.S. Treasury high-grade spread). Rates tell you what a loan costs; the survey and the spread tell you how willing lenders are to make one.
  • Value cushion is the average San Francisco Bay Area apartment cap rate from CoStar (a building's yearly income divided by its price) minus the 10-year Treasury yield. It scores 0 at a gap of 3 points or more and 100 at 0.5 points or less. Apartment loans are usually priced about 1.5 to 2 points above the Treasury yield, so once the gap falls below about 1.5 points, borrowing costs more than a building earns. Note that the 10-year Treasury yield is not itself a loan rate, so this cushion is a guide to borrowing costs, not a direct comparison with them. Free fallback: price-to-rent. This changes what is measured: price-to-rent shows how high prices are compared with rents, not how building income compares with borrowing costs.
  • Jobs is the same area used in the single-family score.
  • Rental vacancy uses Apartment List's monthly vacancy index for the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas, weighted by population (70%), and its change from a year earlier (30%). It counts buildings listed on Apartment List, which are mostly professionally managed, so it can differ from other vacancy measures. If it is missing: the U.S. Census Bureau's yearly California rental vacancy rate, compared with its own 2005–2025 average.
  • Rent growth blends the change over the past year in the typical rent on new leases in the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas (Apartment List, 50%) with rent paid by all tenants in the San Francisco–Oakland area (consumer price index, 50%). New-lease rents show today's market; rent paid shows what owners actually collect across all leases. If the Apartment List figure is missing, rent paid is used on its own. Not every owner can raise rents at market pace: California's AB 1482 and local rules such as San Jose's Apartment Rent Ordinance can limit increases.
  • New construction compares homes permitted over the past 24 months in the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas with the average 24-month pace since 2005 (100 = average). Permits come before construction, so this shows supply on the way. Permits cover all private housing, not only apartments.

Levels that would push the score higher

  • The 10-year Treasury yield above 5.25%, or banks broadly tightening standards for apartment loans.
  • Rent growth slowing below 1% or turning negative.
  • San Francisco Bay Area apartment vacancy rising above 5.5%, or cap rates falling to within 0.5 points of the 10-year Treasury yield.
  • Building permits above 130 (30% faster than the usual pace).
  • San Francisco Bay Area unemployment above 5.5%, or tech jobs down more than 3%.

Additional Market Signals Worth Watching

Some indicators are useful for understanding the market but are not included in the final score.

  • Layoff Notices. Tracks reported layoffs across major San Francisco Bay Area counties. A rise in notices may be an early warning of weaker employment. Notices are planned layoffs, not completed job losses, and some are changed or canceled.
  • Insurance Costs. Shows how insurance costs are changing. Higher insurance premiums can reduce the income property owners keep from their rentals.
  • Market History. Shows how today's score compares with previous market conditions, including historical highs and lows.
  • Rent-to-Income. A direct regional measure of rent compared with household income would be useful. However, we have not found a sufficiently reliable monthly data source to include it in the score.

What You Should Keep in Mind

The Rental Risk Gauge brings together several useful market indicators, but no single score can capture every factor affecting a rental property.

  • Some data is updated daily, while other information is monthly or quarterly.
  • Employment and economic data can be revised after publication.
  • Housing markets can change seasonally.
  • Market conditions can vary significantly between cities and neighborhoods.
  • Every property has different financing, expenses, tenants and rental conditions.
  • The scoring rules and weights represent our own methodology, not an industry standard.

The score is designed to provide a useful market overview, not a complete assessment of an individual property.

What Previous Market Cycles Can Teach Us

We applied today's scoring methodology to historical data going back to 2000 to see how the Rental Risk Gauge would have behaved during previous market cycles.

The historical results include several important periods:

  • The dot-com downturn in the early 2000s.
  • The 2008 financial crisis.
  • The relatively stable housing market from 2013 to 2019.
  • The COVID-19 market shock in 2020.
  • The interest-rate increases and housing market slowdown beginning in 2022.

These comparisons help put today's market conditions into perspective.

What the Past Tells Us

Different market downturns affect rental property owners in different ways.

Higher interest rates alone do not necessarily mean a serious downturn. Employment, rental demand, vacancy, lending conditions and property values all matter.

The purpose of historical analysis is to understand how these factors behaved together, not to predict the next market cycle.

Show the scores in each period

Single-family homes & condos

  • Highest score: 67.5 (High), in April 2009. The score was High from October 2008 to May 2009, when jobs, prices and credit all turned against owners at once.
  • 2001–2003: about 45–66 (Elevated, briefly High). The dot-com bust. Tech jobs fell nearly 18% in a year and unemployment more than doubled. The score went from about 27 in January 2001 to 66 in February 2002. Mortgage rates then fell to record lows, and prices kept rising.
  • 2006–2007: about 44–59 (mostly Elevated). Jobs were strong, but prices were far above rents and starting to fall, and mortgage rates had risen.
  • 2013–2019: mostly 10–28 (Low). Calm markets, low mortgage rates, strong job growth and rising prices. The lowest reading was about 10 in January 2018.
  • April 2020: about 50 (Elevated). Unemployment jumped above 13% during the COVID shutdown, while very low mortgage rates and rising prices offset part of the shock.
  • 2023 to now: mostly 38–56 (Moderate to Elevated). Mortgage rates more than doubled while prices fell, with a peak of about 56 in late 2023.

Apartment buildings (5+ units)

  • 2001–2002: rose from about 41 to 64 (Elevated) as tech jobs disappeared and rent growth stopped.
  • 2003–2006: about 60–68 (often High). San Francisco Bay Area rents fell for about two years, vacancy rose above normal and prices ran far ahead of rents.
  • 2009–2010: about 45–60 (Elevated), as job losses hit and rents fell.
  • 2012–2019: mostly 23–42 (Low to Moderate). Rents rose quickly and vacancy fell. The lowest reading was about 23 in early 2015.
  • Mid-2020 to early 2021: about 67–71 (High). During COVID, apartment vacancy in the San Francisco and San Jose metro areas nearly doubled, to almost 9%, and rents on new leases fell about 14%. The highest apartment reading was 71 in March 2021.
  • 2024: about 51–64 (Elevated), with high Treasury yields, slow rent growth and stretched valuations.

From 2018 on, the apartment history uses the same Apartment List vacancy and rent figures as today's score. Before 2018 it uses California rental vacancy and rent paid, which change more slowly. The October 2026 monthly value is about 45.

Why These Risk Categories?

The four risk categories are based on how the indicators behaved during previous market cycles.

We compared historical scores with what happened to home prices and rental conditions afterward to help establish meaningful ranges.

The categories are designed to make it easier to understand the difference between relatively calm conditions and periods of greater financial pressure.

They are not official industry ratings or guarantees of future market performance.

Show how many months fell in each category

Since 2000, single-family homes & condos were Low in 112 months, Moderate in 79, Elevated in 124 and High in 7 (February 2002 and 2008–09). Apartment buildings (5+ units) were Low in 80 months, Moderate in 70, Elevated in 136 and High in 36 (2003–06, after rents fell in the dot-com bust, and mid-2020 to early 2021 during COVID). Elevated is common because the San Francisco Bay Area has had long stretches of real pressure: 2001–2004, 2006–2011 and again since 2022.

Testing the Score Against Historical Market Data

A useful market indicator should be tested against what actually happened in the past.

We applied the current scoring rules to historical data and compared the resulting scores with changes in home prices and rents over the following 12 months.

What We Found

Historically, lower scores generally appeared during periods of stronger market conditions.

Higher scores were more common during periods when property owners faced greater financial pressure.

However, the relationship was not perfect. Some periods with elevated scores were followed by rising home prices, while some high-risk readings occurred after prices had already fallen.

About the High category: The High category has very few historical observations. During the 2008 downturn, the score reached its highest levels after home prices had already fallen substantially. The score measures market stress, not the direction of future prices.

How Sensitive Is the Score to the Weights?

The single-family score stayed Elevated every time. The apartment score stayed Elevated about 4 times out of 5 and was Moderate the rest of the time.
